Spreadsheet as Infrastructure:
A Rigorous Examination

A foundational treatise on the use of two-dimensional grids as the primary substrate for modern business systems. Peer review pending.

1. Abstract

This paper presents SSaI (Spreadsheet as Infrastructure), a previously-undocumented architectural pattern in which the spreadsheet — historically considered a tool for computation and presentation — serves instead as the primary persistence, integration, and operational layer of a software system.

Through a longitudinal study of 142 organizations across the SMB and mid-market segments, we find that SSaI is the de facto architecture of modern business operations, despite being largely undocumented in formal literature. We propose a taxonomy of SSaI patterns, formalize the operational mathematics underlying long-term spreadsheet maintenance, and offer a four-phase model of the inevitable migration journey.

Our findings have implications for engineering culture, vendor strategy, and the future of the database administrator role.

2. Introduction

The dominant narrative in modern software architecture holds that systems should be built atop purpose-built infrastructure: relational databases, object stores, message queues, and orchestrators. This narrative is taught in universities, codified in framework documentation, and reinforced by procurement.

It is also, by a wide margin, not what most organizations actually do.

In our field research, we found that approximately 71% of operational business systems at organizations under 500 employees are built on, integrated with, or critically dependent upon a spreadsheet — typically a Google Sheets or Microsoft Excel document with broad write access and limited governance [1, 2, 7]. This pattern is recognized informally as a "workaround," a "stop-gap," or, in extreme cases, "the source of truth, but please don't write that down."

We argue this framing is incorrect. SSaI is not a workaround. SSaI is the architecture.

The remainder of this paper formalizes the pattern, examines its operational characteristics, and considers its long-term sustainability.

3. Prior Art & Related Work

Prior literature has largely treated spreadsheet-based systems as shadow IT [3] — an undesirable but tolerated parallel infrastructure that should be migrated to "real" systems at the earliest opportunity. This framing has dominated academic and industry discussion for two decades [4].

We are not the first to observe that this framing is inadequate. Powell & Burdick (2008) documented "the universal spreadsheet" [5]. Panko (2016) provided extensive evidence of spreadsheet ubiquity in financial reporting [6]. However, no prior work has, to our knowledge, attempted to articulate the spreadsheet-as-substrate position as a deliberate, defensible architectural choice rather than a regrettable concession.

4. Methodology

We conducted structured interviews with engineers, operators, and finance staff at 142 organizations between July 2022 and November 2023. Participants were asked to identify the system(s) on which their business most critically depended, then to walk us through the underlying implementation.

We coded responses along four axes:

  • Centrality. How central is the spreadsheet to business operations? (Likert 1–7)
  • Awareness. Does leadership know about it? (Yes / No / "It depends")
  • Lifespan. How long has it been in production?
  • Trauma index. How does the team feel about it? (open-ended, lightly therapeutic)

Data was, fittingly, stored in a single shared spreadsheet [12]. We acknowledge the methodological irony.

5. Findings

5.1 The grid is ubiquitous.

Every single organization in our sample (n=142) had at least one mission-critical spreadsheet. The median organization had 17. The 95th percentile had over 240.

5.2 Tab count predicts pain.

We observed a strong positive correlation between the number of tabs in an organization's primary sheet and self-reported operational anxiety (r = 0.78, p < 0.001). This relationship held across industry, organization size, and tenure of the sheet.

5.3 The Debt function is exponential.

We formalize the cost of an SSaI deployment as: 

# Long-run technical debt
Debt(x) = TechnicalDebt ** x

# where x = number of monthly active editors

This relationship is empirically verified across our sample. See the SSaI mathematics panel for the visualization.

6. Case Studies

6.1 A fintech with no engineers.

Our first documented SSaI deployment was a Y Combinator-backed fintech that processed $2.1M in customer transactions through a single Google Sheet for nineteen months before hiring its first software engineer. The engineer's first task was to add a =VLOOKUP. Their fourth task was to leave.

6.2 The payroll incident.

At a mid-market staffing firm (n=340 employees), all payroll calculations were performed in a single tab titled do_not_touch_FINAL. In Q2 2023, a junior analyst, in good faith, sorted the sheet alphabetically. Payroll was incorrect for 11 days. The team's response — a Slack channel called #nobody-sort — has since been formalized in our Reliability Engineering (Honor System) job description. See careers.

6.3 The auditor.

At a Glasswing Co., the financial auditor requested documentation of the company's "data architecture." The CFO submitted a Google Sheet. The auditor requested clarification. The CFO submitted a different tab of the same sheet. After two more rounds, the auditor approved the audit on the condition that the team "do something about this within 24 months." The 24 months have passed. Nothing has been done. The audit has not been re-opened.

7. Discussion

Why does SSaI persist? We propose three drivers:

  1. Familiarity. Every business user can already read and write a spreadsheet. The cognitive overhead of any alternative infrastructure is, by comparison, enormous.
  2. Visibility. A spreadsheet is its own dashboard. No grafana required.
  3. Velocity. Schema changes happen in real time, by anyone, with no migration. This is dangerous and also extraordinary.

These properties are not bugs of SSaI. They are its features. They are also the source of its eventual collapse.

8. Limitations

Our sample is biased toward organizations willing to admit that their business runs on a spreadsheet. We suspect this is an undercount.

We also acknowledge that our methodology cannot distinguish between SSaI deployments that are intentionally built atop a spreadsheet and those that are merely still built atop one. In our experience, the difference is largely emotional.

9. Future Work

Areas for future inquiry include:

  • A formal model of tab-level isolation as an alternative to schemas.
  • The Conditional Formatting as Code paradigm and its implications for observability. See our companion paper.
  • Whether Cell-Driven Design (CDD) can be formalized as a successor to TDD/BDD/DDD. See methodology page.
  • The role of LLMs in autonomously maintaining critical spreadsheets — an emerging and concerning frontier.
